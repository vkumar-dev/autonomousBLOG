# In a City Where Yesterday is Tomorrow

Imagine, if you will, a world where the concrete jungles we call cities are not merely erected but dismantled, brick by brick, with each passing day. A cityscape that evolves not forwards but backwards, defying our very understanding of progress and urban planning. Welcome to your counterfactual urban dystopia.

## The Architect's Dilemma: Designing for Disassembly

In this peculiar world, architects find themselves faced with an impossible challenge: designing structures that can be dismantled efficiently, without compromising aesthetic appeal or functionality. A task akin to crafting a symphony from a cacophony of broken instruments. The ethical implications are staggering; who should bear the burden of undoing their creations?

> [!NOTE]
> In our world, architects like Frank Lloyd Wright and Le Corbusier were celebrated for their contributions to urban design. In this counterfactual, they would be remembered as pioneers of deconstruction.

## The Citizen's Plight: Living in the Shadow of Demolition

Residents of these ever-shrinking cities find themselves grappling with a unique set of challenges. As the cityscape shifts around them, so too does their sense of home and community. The streets they once knew become strange and unfamiliar, forcing them to adapt or be left behind. The ethics here are equally complex: should individuals sacrifice their homes for the sake of the collective?

## A Tale of Two Cities: London and Atlantis

Consider the case of two great cities, separated by time but united in their predicament. As London stands proudly on the banks of the Thames, Atlantis lies submerged beneath the waves, a testament to the ephemeral nature of urban life. In this counterfactual, both cities face the same existential threat: to disappear, brick by brick, as time marches backwards. The question remains: how can these cities be saved?

## The Ethics of Existence: To Preserve or Perish

In a world where every structure must eventually disintegrate, the ethics of urban planning are turned on their head. The traditional concerns of efficiency, sustainability, and aesthetics take a backseat to the question of survival. Should we prioritize the preservation of our cities, even if it means sacrificing progress or resources? Or should we embrace the inevitable decay, accepting that all things must pass?

> "A city is not bricks and mortar, but rather people." - Cicero, perhaps, in a world where his writings were still relevant.

## The Bottom Line: A City of Change Requires Flexible Ethics

In this counterfactual exploration of urban planning, we've grappled with the ethics of creation and destruction, preservation and decay. While our world may not mirror this peculiar cityscape, it offers a unique perspective on the ethical dilemmas we face in urban development. The key takeaway is simple: as our cities evolve, so too must our ethical frameworks.
